This is not a post about productivity systems. 
No five AM morning routines, no inbox zero, no tips on running better meetings.

This is about the other things. 
The things you only realize matter after you accidentally did them once. 
The unwritten rules of work that nobody tells you because they assume you'll figure them out on your own.

I figured them out. Sometimes the hard way, sometimes just by saying yes to the most random questions.

## Say good morning. To everyone.

Not just to your team. Not just to people whose badge is more senior than yours.

To the people at reception. To the person who sets up the coffee in the morning. To the cleaning staff. To whoever.

This sounds like something your mother taught you when you were five. That's because it is. But you'd be surprised how many people stop doing it the moment they step onto the work floor.

What I've noticed: the people at reception know everything. Who's already in, who's running late, what's happening in the building today. They manage meeting rooms, packages, badges for visitors. They are the hub of every office. And if they know you, really know you, not just as that person who sometimes walks by, that becomes the starting point for a lot of other things.

But beyond that: a good morning thrown into the air at nobody in particular isn't much. A good morning with a brief pause and actual eye contact is something else. Do that a few times. The conversations build themselves.

## Just say yes

I work in IT. I'm introverted enough to spend an evening gaming and call that a successful Friday.

And yet, I once said yes when a colleague asked if I wanted to come Pokémon Go on a Saturday. We walked through the rain. Not a little rain, but the kind where you're completely soaked after five minutes and wondering why you're even doing this. We kept playing anyway. We laughed a lot.

Later, I went Pokémon Go with another colleague's kids and partner. If you'd told me a few years earlier that I'd spend a Sunday afternoon in a park catching virtual creatures with the family of someone from work, I wouldn't have believed you. And yet, it was surprisingly great.

Another colleague once asked if I wanted to come play pool. I had never done that. So I went.

These are small things in themselves. A game, a walk, an evening at the pool hall. But looking back, I realize those are exactly the moments when colleagues turn into people you actually get to know. Not during a meeting or at the coffee machine, but somewhere outside the context where you normally see each other.

## Remember one small detail

Kids' names. The dog's name. That someone had a rough week.

At the next conversation, you ask about it. How's X doing? Did that project ever get finished? Is your back feeling better?

People are not used to someone remembering that. They don't expect it. And when it happens anyway, you see something shift in the conversation. A small ease. That feeling of: oh, you were actually listening.

You don't need to keep notes. You don't need a system. Just pay attention and remember one thing. That's enough.

## Don't forget the birthdays

This goes hand in hand with the last one, but it deserves its own section because it's so small and does so much.

If you know someone has a birthday, say it. That's all. No big gesture needed, no cake, no organizing anything. Just stop by or send a message: happy birthday.

It almost feels too simple to mention. But I've seen enough times how someone lights up when it happens anyway, especially when it comes from someone they didn't expect it from. People remember who wished them well. Especially on a day when others forgot.

## Listen. Actually listen. And don't judge.

I work in IT. I write code. I'm the person you call when your application isn't doing what you want or when you're wondering what an API actually is.

And yet I hear things. About divorces in the making. About kids having trouble at school. About workplace conflicts that have been simmering for years. About personal things I have no business knowing and that people tell me anyway.

Why? I think because I listen without judging.

That sounds simple. But try it for real. Without giving advice. Without having a similar story ready to go. Without sharing your opinion unless someone explicitly asks for it.

Just listen, nod, and be there.

The result is that people trust you. And being trustworthy is probably the most underrated soft skill that exists in any workplace.

## Observe before you say anything

When I start at a new client, my first instinct is not to prove myself. My first instinct is to watch.

How do people communicate here? Who listens to whom in a meeting? What gets said and what doesn't? Where do things stall?

You learn more in the first two weeks by quietly observing than in the months after by asking questions at the wrong moments. And when you do say something, you say it with context. You know what's going on. You know who thinks what. You know where to be careful.

People who talk immediately when they arrive somewhere make an impression in the room. People who look first make an impression in the months after. I choose the second.

## Start or take over a community

This is one I did myself, in ways I hadn't entirely planned.

JCast started as a podcast for a handful of people. By now it's a community where interesting people come together around Java, software, and everything in between. And at work, two years ago, I took over JTires: the Java community at my workplace, which already existed but was waiting for a new life.

Both things delivered the same thing: interesting people. People who are curious, who want to build something, who like sharing what they know. Those people are the best colleagues, the best conversation partners, the best connections you can have.

You don't necessarily need to start a podcast or take over a community. An internal newsletter. A lunch-and-learn. A Slack channel about something you care about. The format doesn't matter. It's about bringing people together.

## The secret is that there is no secret

Everything above is just: pay attention to people.

Not networking as a strategy. Not building relationships as career planning. Just being genuinely interested in the person next to you. Saying yes when you'd rather say no but suspect it'll be worth it. Remembering what someone told you. Listening without judging.

The rest follows on its own.
